# Of Sails and the Wind

*How a ship is made to go, and how she is made to stop.*

A ship goes by the wind and by nothing else. The whole art is to set the cloth so that the wind pushes her where you wish to go, and to take the cloth in again when it does her harm.

## The lines

Every sail has a line hanging from a **belaying pin** on a rail beside its mast. The jib and the staysail, and the sails of the foremast, are worked at the rail abaft the foremast. The sails of the mainmast and the spanker are worked at the rail abaft the mainmast.

Take hold of the end of a line and **haul it down**: the sail is gathered up and furled. A square sail goes halfway first, which is called **reefed**, and then all the way. **Ease the line upward** and the sail drops and sets. Let go, and the pin holds whatever you have done.

A strong wind makes the cloth heavy. Hauling takes more work the harder it blows.

## The braces

The square sails hang from yards that swing like gates. Two **braces** on each mast, one on the port bulwark and one on the starboard, turn the yards.

- Haul the port brace and the port end of the yards swings aft. Haul the starboard brace and it goes the other way.
- A sail with the wind coming at it from the side, and filled at a slant, pulls the ship along.
- A sail turned square to the wind has no push in it and only drags. A sail turned too sharp, or pointed too close to the wind, shakes and gives nothing.
- A sail with the wind on its **front** is said to be **aback**, and it pushes the ship **astern**.

The fore-and-aft sails, the jib, the staysail and the spanker, are trimmed by the crew, so a ship with no hands in her will not have these set well.

## Reading the wind

The pennant on its staff by the wheel shows the wind **as the ship feels it**. That is not the same as the wind blowing over the sea. A ship that is moving makes a wind of her own, and the two are added together, so the faster you go the further ahead the wind appears to come.

A pennant hanging limp means a light air. When it flies level, there is a good breeze.

## Storms and the glass

The wind is not the same everywhere, nor at every season. Each part of the world has its own prevailing wind, and its own bad weather: gales run east through the northern seas in winter, hurricanes come down the trade winds in late summer and autumn, cyclones come in from the east when the monsoon turns, and small squalls rise in the calm belt on summer afternoons. The **Ship's Log** tells you the season.

A storm is many kilometres across, and it takes hours to sail out of one. Learn to see it coming:

- A **dark wall** builds on the horizon, with rain under it.
- The **glass** (the barometer) stands on the chart table. In fair weather it reads about **thirty** inches and wanders a little from day to day. When a storm is on its way it **falls**, and it begins to fall long before the wind rises. Below about twenty-nine, rain and wind are near. At twenty-eight and a half the storm is on you. The words are printed on the dial.
- You cannot lie down to sleep with a storm on you while any sail is set, and you will be woken if one closes in while you sleep. **Haul every sail up** (furl them all) and you may sleep through it: she lies a-hull and goes nowhere, and you wake when it is past. Driving out of a storm is still the better course.

Most of the big storms go as fast as a brig or faster, so do not try to run before one. Turn **across** its path and leave it behind.

## Where the wind should be

How fast she goes depends on where the wind is **relative to the ship**. Read it from the pennant, and from the sails. These are rough speeds for a brig in a fresh breeze with all her proper sail set:

- **Wind from dead ahead, or within about forty-five degrees of it:** she cannot sail into it. The sails shake and give nothing.
- **Close to the wind**, about fifty to sixty degrees off the bow: slow, five knots or so, and she leans far over.
- **Wind abeam, from the side:** a good, steady seven or eight knots.
- **Wind on the quarter, from behind and to one side:** her best, eight to ten knots.
- **Wind from dead astern:** seven or eight knots, and less than you would hope, for the forward sails are becalmed behind the others.

She will not go much over **ten knots** whatever the wind does. In a **gale** (thirty knots and more), take in the topgallants and reef the topsails and the courses first, or she will be driven too hard and leaned too far.

To go somewhere the wind will not let you, sail across it first and then across it the other way, bringing her round, so that the wind falls on one side and then the other, working toward the place a little at a time.

## What to expect

- A ship under sail leans over. The taller her sails, the more she leans.
- She also slides a little sideways through the water, which is called **leeway**, and the keel resists this better the faster she goes.
- She wants to turn up into the wind by herself, which is called **weather helm**, and the rudder has to hold her against it.
- A brig will not go much faster than ten knots, however hard it blows.
- With the wind straight from astern, the sails in front sit in the lee of those behind them and take less wind.
