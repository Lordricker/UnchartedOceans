# Of Trade and Cargo

*How a master makes his living: buy where it is cheap, sell where it is dear, and do not sink on the way.*

A ship that carries nothing earns nothing. The owners will not be paid in fine sailing. Cargo is the whole business, and it is simple enough to say: **a thing costs less where it is made than where it is wanted.** Carry it from the one to the other and the difference is yours.

## The market

Every port has stalls along the quay and in the square. Walk up to a stallholder and speak to him: a **grocer**, a **spice trader**, a **cloth merchant**, a **chandler**, a **wine seller**, a **smith** and others. Each deals only in his own kind of goods, so a master who wants cloth must find the cloth merchant.

A stall shows two lists.

- **For sale.** What the stallholder has to sell, with the price of one box, and what a box weighs.
- **We buy.** What he will take from you, out of your hold. He deals in the same kinds of goods that he sells, whether or not the port makes them.

Press **Esc**, or the Leave button, to go out again.

## Reading a price

Beside every price is a word.

- **cheap**, in green. The port values this good low. Buy it here.
- **fair**. An ordinary price.
- **dear**, in red. The port values this good high. Sell it here, and think twice before buying.

The percentage says how far from the usual price it stands. A good marked *cheap -35%* in one port and *dear +40%* in another is a voyage worth making.

Remember three things.

1. **Where a thing is made, it is cheap.** The four great regions of the world each make different goods. Spice is cheap in the east and dear in the west; the clockmakers of the west sell their clocks dear to everyone else.
2. **Great ports are rich.** A capital sells goods it does not make itself, and it keeps the rare and costly wares that no small harbour has. A small port sells only what grows or is made near it.
3. **No two ports quote alike.** Even neighbours in one region differ a little. Look at both prices before you sail.

## Your own trade moves the price

A port is only so large. Every box you **buy** makes that good dearer, and a little of the same kind of goods too. Every box you **sell** makes it cheaper. A great port feels this little. A small port feels it a great deal. So:

- Do not buy a whole port's stock of one good at the price on the first box. The last box costs more than the first.
- Do not sell the whole hold into one small port. The price falls as you go.
- The stallholder buys for less than he sells for, so a master who buys and sells in the same port at once only loses.

Left alone, prices **drift back** toward their usual level, a few days at a time, and faster in a rich port. A master who comes back to a port after a long voyage will find the price he spoiled has mended itself. Time passes while you sleep, and the markets mend with it.

## Buying and loading

What you buy is not carried to the ship for you. It is sent to the **port box**, a strong chest on the quay beside your berth. Look at it and it is outlined, and it tells you what is in it and how many.

1. **Click the port box** with empty hands. The oldest box in it comes out into your hands.
2. **Carry it aboard.** It is held between your hands, low, where you can still see over it. It is awkward but not heavy to you, and it does not slow you.
3. **Hold Space** when you are in the hold. A ghost of the box shows where it would lie: **green** if it will rest there, **red** if it will not. **E** tips it end over end. **R** turns it a quarter flat. Click to set it down.
4. **Right click** sets a box down on the ground wherever you are standing. It is not fixed to anything, and you may take it up again. **Q** sends it back to the port box. Or click the port box itself to put it back.

All the port boxes in the world are one chest, in a manner of speaking. What you buy at one berth may be taken from the port box at another.

## Stowing

A box must **rest on something**: the floor, a step or table, or another box. It need not rest on all of its length, but if you take away what it rests on, it will fall until it finds something. Stairs and the places where the crew's hammocks are slung are not for cargo. **More hands means more hammocks, and so less room for cargo** (see *Of Crew and Hammocks*).

Bales, sacks, barrels, crates and chests all come in different shapes. Packed with care, they fit together. Packed carelessly, they leave holes, and holes carry nothing.

## Selling

Go to a stall that deals in what you carry. Under **We buy** you will see how many of each good you have in the hold, what you paid for them (on average), the price he offers, and whether that is cheap or dear. Choose **Sell 1**, **Sell 5** or **Sell all**.

The dockhands take the boxes straight out of your hold. You need not carry them. The gold is paid at once, and the stallholder tells you whether you made a profit or a loss on what you paid.

Sell the highest boxes first if you like: what rests on them settles when they go.

## Weight, again

Every box has a weight, and the weight is in the price you pay for it. Copper and iron are heavy and fill a hold with little in it. Cloth, spice and silk are light, and many boxes may be carried before the ship feels them. A cargo of a hundred tonnes of grain takes up far more room than a hundred tonnes of silk, and silk is worth more than grain besides.

The ship has **no limit** on what you may load. She has only the sea. The more she carries, the lower she sits. **If she is loaded too heavily, the sea comes over the rails and into the hold, and a ship that fills is lost.** Read *Of Weight and Sinking*. When the rails are close to the water, stop loading. If she begins to take water, sell or throw cargo over the side at once.

## A few rules of the trade

- **Know your prices.** Look in every port you pass, even if you buy nothing. The one you remember may be the one that makes your fortune.
- **Carry light and dear goods far, heavy and cheap goods near.** A long voyage should earn more than a short one.
- **Do not trust a price that is far from the usual.** It will not hold when you come to sell.
- **Keep gold in hand** for stores and wages. A master with a full hold and an empty purse cannot pay his men.
