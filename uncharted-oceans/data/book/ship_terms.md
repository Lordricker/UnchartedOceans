# Of the Ship and Her Words

*A sailor's word-book: the words you will meet aboard, and what each one means for you.*

A ship has a language of her own, and there is a reason for it. Ashore, left and right belong to your body. Aboard, you turn about while the ship turns under you, so a sailor names every place and every direction by the ship and not by himself. Learn the words and they keep you from mistakes. Every other book on this shelf is written in them. Come back here whenever you meet one you do not know.

## Six words to learn first

- **Bow** and **stern**: the front end of the ship and the back end.
- **Port** and **starboard**: her left side and her right side as you face the bow. They never change, however you turn about. Port and left both have four letters.
- **Windward**: the side the wind comes from.
- **Leeward**: the side away from the wind. The **lee** is the sheltered side.
- **Heading**: the way her bow is pointing, read in degrees from the compass.
- **Knot**: a measure of speed, and the one the whole trade is reckoned in.

## Where things lie

- **Fore** or **forward**: toward the bow. **Aft**: toward the stern.
- **Abaft**: behind. The rail abaft the foremast is the rail just behind it, on the stern side.
- **Ahead**: out in front of the bow. **Astern**: out behind the stern.
- **Abeam**: out to the side, at right angles to the ship.
- **Quarter**: the part of her side toward the stern. Wind on the quarter comes from behind and to one side.
- **Amidships**: the middle of the ship. A wheel that is amidships is straight, and so is the rudder.
- **Aloft**: up in the masts and rigging. **Below**: under the deck.

## The hull and the decks

- **Hull**: the body of the ship, the part that floats.
- **Keel**: the great timber along the bottom. On the brig it reaches more than two metres below the water.
- **Rudder**: the flat blade hung at the stern. The wheel turns it, and it turns the ship.
- **Waterline**: where the sea meets the hull. The more she carries, the higher it climbs. Watch it against the rails.
- **Rail** and **bulwark**: the low wall round the edge of the deck. The sail lines are tied off at the rails, and the braces lead to the bulwarks.
- **Main deck**: the long open deck where the masts rise and the guns stand.
- **Poop deck**: the raised deck at the stern. The wheel, the log line and the sand glass are all up here. The name is old and means only the rear of the ship.
- **Captain's cabin**: the room under the poop. Your chart table, bed and bookshelf are here.
- **Main hatch**: the opening in the deck, with the stairs down.
- **Hold**: the space below the deck where cargo is stowed. The crew's hammocks take up part of it.
- **Bilge**: the lowest part of the hold, where leaked water gathers. The **pump** drives it out again.
- **Guns** or **cannon**: the brig carries three a side, firing through the **gun ports** cut in her sides. They are the heaviest things aboard that are not cargo.
- **Entry steps**: the steps up her side, one set on each side.
- **Gangway**: the plank run out to the pier on the port side, between the two forward guns, once she is moored.
- **Capstan**: an upright drum on the deck, turned to haul heavy cable. The **cable** lies coiled beside it.
- **Binnacle**: the stand in front of the wheel that holds the compass.
- **Lookout barrel**: a barrel fixed high on a mast, from which you can see farther than from the deck. Climb the shrouds to reach it.
- **Ensign**: the flag flown at the stern. **Pennant**: the narrow swallow-tailed flag on its staff by the wheel, which shows you the wind.

## Masts and rigging

- **Mast**: an upright pole that carries the sails. The brig has two: the **foremast** forward and the **mainmast** behind it, a little taller.
- **Bowsprit**: the spar that points forward from the bow. The **jib boom** is its extension, farther out.
- **Yard**: a crosswise spar that a square sail hangs from. It swings like a gate.
- **Boom**: the spar along the foot of the spanker.
- **Rigging**: all the rope, chain and spars that hold up the masts and work the sails. There are two kinds.
- **Standing rigging**: the fixed ropes that hold the masts up. **Stays** run from the mastheads toward the bow and the stern. **Shrouds** run down the sides of the masts, and you climb them like a ladder.
- **Running rigging**: the ropes that move. These are the ones you handle: the sail lines and the braces.
- **Line**: a sailor's word for any rope in use. Ashore it is rope. Aboard, a rope that is working is a line.
- **Belaying pin**: a wooden peg set in the rail. A line wound round it is held fast. To **belay** is to make a line fast, and **belay that** is an order to stop.
- **Braces**: the lines that swing the yards round. There are two to each mast, one on each side.
- **Haul**: to pull a line in. **Ease**: to let it out slowly.

## The sails

A **square sail** hangs from a yard across the ship. A **fore-and-aft sail** is set along the length of the ship. The brig has nine sails, and the drawing in *Your Orders* numbers them. From the bow aft:

- **Jib**: the triangle set forward of the foremast, on the stays. Fore-and-aft.
- **Fore staysail**: the triangle just in front of the foremast. Fore-and-aft.
- **Fore course, fore topsail, fore topgallant**: the three square sails on the foremast, from the lowest up to the highest.
- **Main course, main topsail, main topgallant**: the same three on the mainmast.
- **Spanker**: the fore-and-aft sail behind the mainmast, hung from a boom.

The lowest square sail is the **course**. The one above is the **topsail**, and the highest the **topgallant**. The higher a sail stands, the lighter and smaller it is, and the sooner it should come in when the wind rises. In a gale take in the topgallants first.

Mark that **course** has two meanings: the lowest sail on a mast, and the way the ship is going. The sentence tells you which.

On the brig, the lines for the jib, the staysail and the foremast sails are worked at the rail abaft the foremast. The lines for the mainmast and the spanker are worked at the rail abaft the mainmast.

- **Set**: to put a sail to the wind. Ease its line and it falls open.
- **Furl**: to gather a sail up. Haul its line down.
- **Reef**: to take a square sail halfway in, so that it catches less wind. Reef when it blows hard.
- **Drawing**: filled with wind and pulling. **Shaking**: flapping, and giving nothing.
- **Aback**: with the wind on its front, so that it pushes the ship astern.
- **Canvas**: the cloth the sails are made of, and a sailor's word for all of them together.
- **Make sail**: set more canvas. **Shorten sail**: take some in.

## The wind

- **True wind**: the wind blowing over the sea.
- **Apparent wind**: the wind as the moving ship feels it. The pennant shows this one. The faster you go, the further ahead it seems to come.
- **Close-hauled**: sailing as near to the wind as she will go, about fifty to sixty degrees off the bow. Slow, and she leans far over.
- **In irons**: pointing straight into the wind, or within about forty-five degrees of it. The sails shake and give nothing, and she soon loses her way. Turn her away from the wind to get her going again.
- **Beam reach**: wind abeam. A good steady speed.
- **Broad reach**: wind on the quarter. Her best speed.
- **Running**: wind dead astern. Slower than you would think, because the forward sails sit in the lee of the others.
- **Tack**: to work toward the wind by sailing across it one way, bringing the bow through the wind, and sailing across it the other. Each leg is a tack too.
- **Wear**: to turn the other way about, by swinging the stern through the wind. It takes more room than tacking, and it is safer in a gale.
- **Heel**: to lean over under the press of the wind.
- **Leeway**: the sideways slip through the water.
- **Weather helm**: her wish to turn up into the wind. You hold a little wheel against it.
- **Lee shore**: land that the wind is blowing you toward. It is the most dangerous coast there is, because you cannot sail off it. Keep well out.
- **Becalmed**: with no wind to move her.

## Weather

- **Breeze**: a good steady wind for sailing. **Gale**: thirty knots and more. **Squall**: a short, sudden blow with rain.
- **Hurricane** and **cyclone**: great storms of the tropics, which come in their own seasons. Turn across their path and leave them behind.
- **The glass**: the barometer on the chart table. It reads about thirty in fair weather and falls before a storm. At twenty-nine, rain and wind are near. At twenty-eight and a half, the storm is on you.
- **A-hull**: lying with every sail furled, going nowhere, and riding out the storm. Do this only if you cannot outrun it.
- **Harbour breeze**: the wind near a port, which blows out in the morning and in at evening.

Mark that **the glass** has two meanings: the barometer, and the sand glass at the stern rail used with the log. The sentence tells you which.

## Steering and measuring

- **Wheel**: the great wheel that turns the rudder. **Helm** means the wheel and its gear, and a **helmsman** is the man at it.
- **Hard over**: the wheel turned as far as it will go.
- **Steady**: hold her on her heading. **Steady as she goes** is the order to keep it so.
- **Way**: the ship's movement through the water. **Under way** means moving. **Way on** means she has enough speed for the rudder to bite. Without it she will not steer.
- **Compass card**: the dial in the compass, which keeps its north.
- **Lubber's line**: the red mark on the rim, fixed to the ship. The number under it is the heading.
- **Bearing**: the direction to something, in degrees from north, going round with the sun. East is ninety, south one hundred and eighty, west two hundred and seventy.
- **Knot**: one nautical mile in the hour. One knot is 1.852 kilometres in the hour.
- **Nautical mile**: the sailor's mile, 1.852 kilometres.
- **Log** and **log line**: how you learn your speed. Every wooden block that goes over the rail while the glass runs is one knot.
- **Stray line**: the plain length at the start of the log line, which takes the chip clear of the wake. **The chip** is the little board at the end, that stays where it is in the water.
- **Dead reckoning**: finding your place from your heading, your speed and your time, without seeing land.
- **Fathom**: six feet, or about 1.8 metres. Used for depth and for length of line.
- **Lead line**: a line with a lead weight on the end, dropped to measure the depth.
- **Shoal**: shallow water. **Aground**: with the keel on the bottom.
- **Draught**: how deep the hull sits in the water.

## Port and berth

- **Port**: has two meanings, the left side of the ship and a harbour. The sentence tells you which.
- **Pier** or **quay**: the structure along the water that ships lie against.
- **Berth**: the place marked out for a ship to lie, and shown by the outline of light. A berth is cut to the size of the ship meant for it.
- **Moor**: to make the ship fast to the pier. **Mooring** is the whole business.
- **Lines**: in port, the ropes that tie the ship to the pier. You **put the lines ashore**.
- **Bollard**: the short post on the pier that the lines are tied to.
- **Fender**: padding between the hull and the pier, so the ship does not grind against it.
- **Cast off**: to let the lines go and drift clear of the pier.
- **Harbour crane**: the crane on the quay that swings heavy cargo aboard.

## Crew, cargo and trade

- **Master**: the captain. That is you.
- **Hands**: the crew. Each man has a **hammock** to sleep in.
- **Prize**: a ship you have taken. A **prize crew** is the men you put aboard her to sail her.
- **Provisions** or **stores**: the food and drink for the voyage.
- **Chandler**: a trader who sells ships' stores.
- **Cargo**: what you carry to sell. A **box** is the unit of it.
- **Port box**: the strong chest on the quay at your berth, where what you buy is sent.
- **Stow**: to pack cargo in the hold so that it lies safe and fits together.
- **Cheap**, **fair**, **dear**: the price words at a stall. Dear means costly, not beloved.
- **Awash**: with the water level with the rails.
- **Founder**: to fill with water and sink.
- **Plug**: to stop a hole in the hull.
- **Jettison**: to throw cargo over the side to lighten her.

## What you may hear said

- **Aye aye**: I have heard the order, and I will do it.
- **Avast**: stop.
- **Heave to**: stop the ship by setting the sails against each other so that she holds still.
- **Fair wind**: a wind that carries you where you want to go.
- **Land ho**: land has been sighted.
- **Hands to the braces**: all hands to turn the yards.
- **Hard a-port**, **hard a-starboard**: put the wheel hard over to that side.
- **Shipshape**: neat, in good order, ready.
