# Of the Compass and the Stars

*How a master knows where he is when there is nothing to see.*

Out of sight of land there is no mark to steer by, and the sea looks the same in every direction. A master finds his place by **reckoning**, and by the stars when he can.

## Dead reckoning

Three things are needed, and the ship gives them all:

1. **The course**, from the compass in the binnacle.
2. **The speed**, from the log and line (see *Of the Log and Line*).
3. **The time** sailed on that course.

Speed multiplied by time gives the distance run. Lay it off along the course from where you started and you have your place. A ship that makes **5 knots** for **3 hours** has gone **15 nautical miles**, which is **28 kilometres** on the chart (a knot is 1.852 km an hour; see *Of the Chart and of Sleep*).

Mark the new place on the chart in the cabin. Do it often, and every time the course or the speed changes.

## Why you can never be sure

The reckoning is never exact. The log gives the speed through the **water**, and a current may be carrying ship and water together, which the log will never show. The compass card may be a degree or two out. Wind and sea push the ship off her course between your readings, and a hasty glass gives a false speed.

On the chart the **ring** about your ship shows this. The longer you are away from the last port, the wider it grows. A few hours out it is small. After days on open water it may be larger than the island you look for.

## The stars

On a clear night the stars do not drift. A master who can find his latitude by them has a fixed place to start from, and the ring of doubt shrinks to nothing. This is the **hard** way and asks more of the navigator.

## Hiring a pilot

A sailor who knows these waters can be hired in a port tavern and will come aboard with you. Ask him, and he will point toward the port you seek, and tell you how far it lies. He will not sail the ship for you, and he can be wrong.
