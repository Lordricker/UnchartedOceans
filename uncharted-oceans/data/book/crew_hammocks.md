# Of Crew and Hammocks

*How many hands a ship wants, and where she puts them.*

## Hands

A ship sails with fewer men than she needs and does it badly. The brig can be sailed by a **single man** if he works every line himself, but each sail he sets takes him time, for he must walk from rope to rope. A crew of **eight to ten** sails her well. She will carry **twelve** at the most, and every ship has a smallest crew she can be trusted to. For the brig it is **five**.

More hands also work faster. One man raises one sail at a time. A larger crew raises several at once.

## Where they sleep

Each sailor has a **hammock**, and the hammocks are slung in sets of three between posts. The number of hammocks aboard is the number of sailors she can carry. The brig has room for only so many sets, and more hammocks than she needs would leave no room for cargo.

A hammock takes up space that cargo would otherwise have. **A ship with a small crew carries more cargo.** A ship with a large crew carries less, but sails better and is harder to board.

The hammocks swing a little as the ship moves. This is as it should be.

## What they eat

Every man eats. **More men need more provisions**, and a ship that sets out with too few will have a hungry crew within days. Plan the stores for the length of the voyage and a little more.

## When a prize is taken

A ship you have taken is not sailed home by you. Put a **prize crew** aboard her, and she follows. A prize needs at least her own minimum crew. Without them she will not stay with you, and you will have gained nothing by taking her.
